what it does

≈ The Z layer

Press z and the record drops to a whisper while a second sheet comes up over it, in exact register, carrying your own marks. Lower it and there is nothing but the conversation again. Annotation is usually something you do to a record; here it is a separate plane that never touches it.

What makes it more than a nice gesture: a mark is stored as a moment and a height — never a pixel, never a message id. Drag the track from five years down to ninety days and your handwriting stretches with the years, because it reads the same map the conversation does. An annotation anchored to time rather than to a document is one that can outlive the document.

📌 The pin becomes a ledger

Send 📌, 🔥, or ❓ in your messages like you already do — Zettel turns each into a glowing marker on your timeline and a row in a living decision ledger. Declare a fresh decision and it writes back into the real thread; you both see it. Then copy the minutes — the whole ledger drops out as clean markdown, ready for the doc.

🔎 Search that actually works

Everything Apple's search forgets: "exact phrase" · from:them · after:2024-01-01 · has:link · in:all — across one thread or every conversation you've ever had. Even words that were only spoken: voice memos transcribe on your own machine.

≈ The question that came back

Zettel proposes where to look using structure alone — a question mark, then a fortnight of silence — without reading a word of what was said. Then, in your browser and nowhere else, it checks whether two of those silences were the same sentence. When someone asks you something in 2023 and asks it again in 2026, and neither time got an answer, the sheet ties the two together with a line. Nothing searched for it.

〰️ A decade as one waveform

Your whole thread as a track — your voice above the line, theirs below. Scrub years in a second, name the chapters, press space and it plays itself. The instrument your most important conversation never had.

By default, nothing leaves your Mac — no account, no server of ours, and the archive is read-only. Your margins stay private on your machine. Turn the wifi off — everything still works.

Three exceptions exist, each a door only you can open. Your wifi: --share serves your archive to a device on your own network, over plain HTTP — a wifi you trust, never one you share with strangers. Your folder: if you and one other person choose to co-annotate a thread, only the notes you each explicitly promote sync — through a folder in a cloud you choose and control; those bytes pass through that company's servers, not ours, and revoking the folder ends it. Your summons: when you call on an outside mind — a question to Claude, a parcel tracker, a forecast — exactly what you selected travels to that one service, and the summons shows you the payload before it goes. Nothing is scanned, watched, or sent without your two taps.

Every exception is a loan, not an amendment: as capability comes home to your Mac, the crossings retire.

getting it onto your Mac

Zettel isn't in the App Store — it can't be; Apple sandboxes App Store apps away from your Messages archive, which is the whole point. So it's a small, open, notarization-pending download, and it goes out with early access rather than sitting on a public link. So you know what you're agreeing to, this is the whole of it:

  1. Download and unzip zettel.zip.
  2. Right-click Zettel.command → Open → Open. (Right-click, not double-click — macOS blocks unsigned apps opened the normal way. This is the one awkward step until we notarize.)
  3. Grant Terminal Full Disk Access when asked (System Settings → Privacy & Security → Full Disk Access → add Terminal), then run it once more. This is macOS asking your permission to read your messages — nothing is sent anywhere.
  4. Needs Python 3 (most Macs have it; if not, the launcher tells you where to get it).
